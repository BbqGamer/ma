\chapter{Introduction}
\label{chap:introduction}

Training deep neural networks is still difficult when the optimization surface
is highly non-convex and contains many poor local minima. In most practical
pipelines, the objective is fixed from the first to the last epoch. Curriculum
learning offers a different view: instead of exposing the full difficulty at
once, training can start from an easier objective and progressively move toward
the final target \citep{elman1993learning,bengio2009curriculum}.

This thesis studies objective-space curricula, with particular focus on
Gaussian continuation. Continuation methods smooth the objective early and then
gradually remove smoothing \citep{allgower1990numerical,mobahi2015theoretical}.
In neural networks this idea is promising, but schedule design is still mostly
manual \citep{wang2021survey}. The central problem addressed here is therefore
how to design and evaluate curriculum schedules that are both effective and
scientifically defensible.

The work combines two complementary perspectives. The first is an
objective-space curriculum track on synthetic non-convex regression surfaces,
inspired by Gaussian continuation. The second is an output-space coarse-to-fine
curriculum track in image classification, introduced to test whether curricula
are more effective when intermediate tasks preserve semantic structure. Across
both tracks, the thesis compares fixed schedules, adaptive schedules, and
hierarchy variants, and places equal emphasis on positive and negative outcomes.
Meta-optimization is evaluated as one route to adaptive scheduling, not assumed
to be the final answer: the experiments find it feasible and interpretable, but
not yet robust enough to replace tuned direct or fixed-schedule baselines.

The motivation for using two tracks is methodological. A single positive result
would not be enough to understand curriculum learning, because many mechanisms
can hide behind the same word ``curriculum.'' A smoothed regression target, a
coarse image label, a teacher-ranked example order, and a meta-learned loss
weight all make the early problem easier, but they do so in different ways. Some
remove information, some aggregate information, and some change only the order
in which information is presented. The thesis therefore treats curriculum
learning not as one algorithm but as a family of interventions on the training
objective.

This distinction matters for evaluation. If the early task removes information
that is needed later, then the curriculum may only delay learning. If the early
task preserves useful structure, then it may guide the model toward a better
representation. Similarly, an adaptive schedule can help only if the progress
signal it uses is aligned with the final objective. Otherwise, the scheduler may
stay too long on an easy stage or move too early to a hard one. These are not
implementation details; they determine whether a curriculum is a useful training
strategy or just an expensive detour.

A second motivation is the role of controls. Curriculum learning is vulnerable
to overly optimistic comparisons. A method can look effective because the direct
baseline is weak, because the curriculum uses more compute, because the best
checkpoint is selected retrospectively, or because label coarsening acts as a
regularizer even when the hierarchy is random. For this reason, the thesis puts
unusual emphasis on negative and diagnostic comparisons: tuned baselines,
runtime checks, random hierarchy controls, reverse-order controls, and
trajectory metrics. The aim is to identify when curriculum learning actually
adds useful structure rather than merely changing the training protocol.

The resulting thesis is therefore empirical but not only benchmark-driven. The
experiments are used to refine the claim. The final conclusion is not that
curriculum learning is universally beneficial. It is that curriculum learning can
help in regimes where the intermediate task is aligned with the final task, the
model has limited capacity, and the schedule gives enough but not too much time
to easier stages.

\section{Research questions}

The empirical part is organized around four research questions. They are phrased
so that both positive and negative outcomes are informative.

\begin{description}
    \item[RQ1.]\phantomsection\label{rq:continuation} Does objective-space
          continuation improve non-convex regression tasks after the direct
          single-target baseline is strongly tuned?
    \item[RQ2.]\phantomsection\label{rq:vision} Does output-space coarse-to-fine
          supervision improve image classification, and how does the effect
          depend on model capacity, dataset structure, curriculum length, and
          hierarchy depth?
    \item[RQ3.]\phantomsection\label{rq:adaptive} Can adaptive or meta-optimized
          schedules replace fixed hand-designed curricula, or do they introduce
          new failure modes such as staying too long on easy objectives?
    \item[RQ4.]\phantomsection\label{rq:diagnostics} Do trajectory, roughness,
          and sharpness diagnostics support a clear optimization mechanism, or do
          they only provide correlational evidence in selected positive cases?
\end{description}

\section{Contributions}

The main contributions of the thesis are:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item A reproducible training and analysis pipeline for dynamic multi-loss
          curriculum experiments in both regression and image-classification
          settings.
    \item A broad empirical study showing that curriculum effects are real but
          strongly conditional: they depend on capacity, schedule, hierarchy
          quality, and dataset structure.
    \item An adaptive plateau curriculum variant and hierarchy-control suite
          including matched random hierarchies, teacher-derived hierarchies, and
          reverse-order curricula, separating label coarsening from hierarchy
          structure and temporal order.
    \item A critical synthesis that includes mixed and negative results, not only
          best-case improvements.
\end{enumerate}

\chapter{Background and related work}
\label{chap:literature-review}

This chapter reviews the literature behind the methodological choices in this
thesis. The discussion focuses on curriculum design, output-space label
curricula, multi-objective optimization, dynamic reweighting, and meta-learning.
The goal is not to provide an exhaustive survey, but to identify which ideas are
mature, which assumptions are often hidden, and where the open methodological
gaps remain.

\section{Curriculum learning families}
\label{sec:curriculum-continuation}

The idea that models benefit from learning tasks in order of increasing
difficulty predates modern deep learning. \citet{elman1993learning} showed
that neural networks with limited capacity could learn complex structures if
they started with simpler tasks. These early constraints are not just shortcuts,
but important for guiding the learning process.

\subsection{Foundational curriculum learning}
\citet{bengio2009curriculum} formalized curriculum learning as an
easy-to-hard training strategy and connected it to continuation methods: early
objectives or data distributions can be viewed as smoother versions of the final
problem. Later examples made this idea concrete in different ways. \citet{spitkovsky2010baby} used ``Baby Steps'' and ``Leapfrog'' schedules in
unsupervised dependency parsing, showing that progressively increasing data
complexity can improve a non-convex learning procedure. \citet{kumar2010selfpaced} introduced self-paced learning, where the model and
sample-selection variables are optimized together so that easy samples enter the
objective first. \citet{zaremba2014learning} showed in
neural program execution that the precise curriculum matters: a conventional
schedule was not enough, but a task-specific curriculum made difficult sequence
problems learnable. \citet{graves2017automated} then framed the
problem as automated syllabus selection, using learning-progress signals to
choose what the network should train on next.

These works establish three ideas used throughout this thesis. First, a
curriculum can change the data distribution, the task definition, or the loss,
not merely the order of mini-batches. Second, the notion of ``easy'' is often
model-dependent rather than a fixed human label. Third, a curriculum is a
schedule-design problem: the same intermediate task can help, do nothing, or
hurt depending on when it is introduced and when it is removed. Recent surveys
therefore distinguish instance-based curricula, which sort or weight examples,
from objective-based curricula, which change the training objective itself
\citep{soviany2022curriculum,wang2021survey}.

\citet{weinshall2018curriculum} give a sharper theoretical
argument for instance-based curriculum learning. In a linear-regression setting
with SGD, they define example difficulty using the loss under the optimal
hypothesis rather than the loss under the current model. This is a theoretical
device, not a directly usable training signal: the optimal hypothesis is unknown
during learning, and if it were already available the original
supervised-learning problem would essentially have been solved. Under this
oracle definition, easier examples yield larger expected progress toward the
optimum, especially early in training when the model is still far from a good
solution. Their analysis also separates inherent difficulty from current loss:
among examples of equal inherent difficulty, high current-loss points can still
be informative, but blindly prioritizing all high-loss examples can be harmful
because some are intrinsically noisy or ambiguous. This distinction is useful
for this thesis because it clarifies why curriculum learning and hard-example
mining are not the same strategy.

The practical contribution of Weinshall et al.\ is therefore to replace the
unobservable oracle ordering with a proxy. They estimate example difficulty with
a separate pretrained teacher model and use that ranking to drive an
easy-to-hard schedule. Their CNN experiments show the strongest gains in early
optimization, with more conditional improvements in final accuracy when the task
is harder, the student network is smaller, or regularization is stronger. The
paper therefore supports a cautious interpretation that aligns with this thesis:
curricula often act by stabilizing the early optimization trajectory, but their
final generalization benefit depends on regime and design choices. At the same
time, their theory is still limited to convex linear regression and the
teacher-derived difficulty score is only a proxy, so the result is best treated
as motivation rather than a complete explanation of curriculum effects in deep
networks.

\subsection{Objective-space curriculum and continuation}
Objective-space curricula change the definition of the task or loss rather than
only changing which examples are emphasized. They are closely linked to
numerical continuation methods \citep{allgower1990numerical}, which are used in
global optimization. In these methods, training starts with a smoothed, convex
version of the objective function, making it easier to find a good solution. As
training continues, the smoothing is gradually reduced, allowing the model to
tackle more complex aspects of the problem.

\subsection{Gaussian continuation}
One practical way to apply continuation methods in deep learning is through
Gaussian continuation. \citet{mobahi2015theoretical} analyze
this idea theoretically by embedding a non-convex objective into a family of
Gaussian-smoothed objectives and following a stationary path from the smoothed
problem back to the original one. Their result does not claim that continuation
always finds the global optimum. Instead, it bounds the value reached by the
continuation path using an ``optimization complexity'' term of the objective.
This is important for this thesis because it gives a formal reason why smoothing
can help: objectives with lower continuation complexity have better worst-case
bounds under this procedure. However, the calculable part of this theory is
narrow. Mobahi and Fisher compute the complexity analytically for objectives
represented with Gaussian radial basis functions. That function family is useful
for theory and for illustrating Gaussian smoothing, but it is much less general
than modern neural-network training objectives. In particular, there is no
straightforward way to compute the same complexity measure for a stochastic,
high-dimensional neural-network loss. The result is therefore used here as a
conceptual justification for continuation, not as a practical diagnostic that can
be evaluated for the neural models in this thesis.

Later work by \citet{ilersich2025deep} provided further evidence
that Gaussian continuation can transform difficult non-convex landscapes into
ones that are easier to optimize. Empirical studies, such as curriculum by
smoothing \citep{sinha2020curriculum}, use the same intuition in learning
systems: early training suppresses high-frequency or noisy parts of the loss,
and later training restores the full objective. This literature supports the
main design choice in the Gaussian-continuation experiments in this thesis, but
it also highlights a limitation: the smoothing schedule is itself a crucial
algorithmic choice, not a detail that can be ignored.

\subsection{Curriculum regularization in scientific machine learning}
A related example of objective-based curriculum appears in physics-informed
neural networks. \citet{krishnapriyan2021characterizing} showed that standard PINNs can fail on convection, reaction, and
reaction-diffusion problems even when the neural network architecture is
expressive enough to represent the solution. Their diagnosis is especially
relevant here: the PDE residual used as a soft regularizer can make the loss
landscape more ill-conditioned and harder to optimize. The failure is therefore
not only a matter of model capacity, but also of how the objective is presented
during training.

Their proposed curriculum regularization starts with an easier PDE constraint,
for example a smaller convection or reaction coefficient, and then gradually
increases the coefficient until the target problem is reached. This is a form of
continuation in the objective itself rather than in the data order. On the
reported PINN benchmarks, this curriculum reduced error by one to two orders of
magnitude in regimes where regular PINN training often failed. The connection to
this thesis is conceptual rather than experimental: both approaches treat the
training objective as something that should be introduced progressively. The PINN
results strengthen the motivation for dynamic loss schedules, because they show
that a physically meaningful regularization term can be correct in principle and
still be harmful if imposed at full strength from the start.

Recent robust curriculum methods also operate directly at the loss level.
SuperLoss appends a generic adaptive term to existing objectives and
automatically downweights high-loss samples, which improves robustness in noisy
settings without requiring task-specific training pipelines
\citep{castells2020superloss}. At the evaluation level, CurBench established a
unified benchmark and toolkit for curriculum learning across vision, language,
and graph domains, making cross-method comparisons more reproducible
\citep{zhou2024curbench}.

Despite these advances, most existing methods rely on fixed schedules for
adjusting the level of smoothing or regularization. These schedules are often
chosen manually and do not adapt to the model's progress \citep{wang2021survey}.
This limitation motivates the development of automated, data-driven scheduling
methods, which is a central focus of this thesis.

\subsection{Output-space curriculum}
Output-space curricula change the supervision target while keeping the input
examples fixed. This makes them different from instance ordering and from
loss-landscape diagnostics: the learner is first asked to solve a coarser label
problem and only later the final fine-label problem.

\citet{stretcuCoarsetoFineCurriculumLearning2021} introduced a
coarse-to-fine curriculum in the output space. Instead of reordering training
examples, they cluster fine labels into progressively finer groups and train the
model from coarse supervision to the final class labels. This is a key
reference for the image-classification experiments in this thesis, especially
for analyzing how hierarchy quality, hierarchy depth, and curriculum length
influence the result.

\subsection{Curriculum learning versus continual learning}

The abbreviation ``CL'' is overloaded: it can mean \emph{curriculum learning}
or \emph{continual learning}. To avoid this ambiguity, this thesis writes both
terms in full in the prose and uses only short table labels such as ``curr.''
when space is limited.

The distinction is important. Curriculum learning is a training strategy for a
chosen target objective: the learner is deliberately exposed to easier examples,
auxiliary tasks, smoother objectives, or coarser labels before returning to the
final target. The intermediate stages are scaffolding. They do not necessarily
need to remain independently solved after the final stage. Continual learning,
by contrast, is usually a problem setting in which the data distribution,
classes, or tasks arrive over time and the learner must acquire new competence
without catastrophically forgetting earlier competence
\citep{kirkpatrick2017overcoming,lopezpaz2017gradient,parisi2019continual}.

The two ideas nevertheless connect naturally. Both involve non-i.i.d. training
trajectories and both raise questions about transfer between tasks, ordering,
and stability versus plasticity. In reinforcement learning, for example,
curriculum learning is often formulated explicitly as the design of a sequence
of source tasks that should improve learning on a target task
\citep{narvekar2020curriculum}. Teacher--student curriculum methods similarly
select tasks according to the learner's progress \citep{matiisen2020teacher}.
This thesis is closer to curriculum learning than to continual learning because
the final target objective is fixed in advance, but continual-learning language
is useful for discussing when the model should preserve or abandon information
from earlier stages.

\section{Multi-task and multi-objective optimization}

To move between objectives of different difficulty, the problem can be set up
as a multi-task learning scenario. Here, the total loss is a weighted sum of
several component losses. However, curriculum learning and multi-task learning
have different goals when it comes to how these tasks interact.

\subsection{Gradient conflict and balancing}
In multi-task learning, the usual goal is to balance progress across all tasks.
One common problem is negative transfer, where learning one task can interfere
with another. Methods like GradNorm \citep{chen2018gradnorm} adjust task weights
to keep gradient magnitudes similar, while PCGrad \citep{yu2020gradient}
modifies gradients to reduce interference. \citet{sener2018multi} proposed the
Multi-Gradient Descent Algorithm (MGDA),
which seeks a solution where no task can be improved without hurting another.
Surveys such as \citet{chen2024gradient} discuss these approaches and note that,
while they help balance tasks, they do not support the sequential learning
needed for curricula.

\subsection{Why balancing is not enough for curricula}
Gradient-balancing methods are designed mainly for simultaneous multi-task
learning: they try to prevent one task from dominating or interfering with the
others \citep{chen2018gradnorm,yu2020gradient,sener2018multi,chen2024gradient}.
Curriculum learning has a different temporal goal. The learner should focus on
an easier task early, then deliberately shift toward the final target rather
than maintain all objectives in equilibrium
\citep{bengio2009curriculum,narvekar2020curriculum,matiisen2020teacher}.

This also separates curriculum learning from continual learning. A continual
learner is usually evaluated on whether it retains old tasks while learning new
ones \citep{parisi2019continual}; a curriculum learner may instead need to reduce
or abandon the auxiliary easy task once it has served its purpose. In this
thesis, the relevant design problem is therefore not only gradient balancing but
controlled transition: when should the optimizer be pushed away from the easy
objective and toward the final target?

\subsection{Pareto continual learning}
Bridging this gap, \citet{lai2025pareto} introduced Pareto Continual
	Learning, which reformulates the stability-plasticity trade-off (retaining old
knowledge vs. learning new tasks) as a Multi-Objective Optimization (MOO)
problem. Instead of finding a single fixed weight, they learn a set of
Pareto-optimal solutions conditioned on a preference vector. This allows the
system to dynamically select the optimal trade-off during inference. Similarly,
\citet{mao2022metaweighting} proposed MetaWeighting, identifying a
critical gap between training loss and generalization loss in MTL. By treating
task weights as hyperparameters optimized via a bi-level optimization loop
(meta-learning), MetaWeighting explicitly minimizes generalization loss. This
distinction is vital for this thesis: we do not merely want to balance the
gradients of the smoothed and sharp losses; we want to schedule them to
maximize final validation performance.

\section{Meta-learning and continual adaptation}
\label{sec:meta-learning-continual}

Meta-learning, or ``learning to learn,'' provides the algorithmic engine to
automate the curriculum schedule. While early meta-learning focused on learning
initializations (e.g., MAML \citep{finn2017model}) for few-shot adaptation, the
field has expanded into Meta-Continual Learning (MCL).

\subsection{Taxonomy of meta-continual frameworks}
A recent survey by \citet{son2024meta} provides a rigorous taxonomy for the
intersection of these fields. They distinguish between \textit{Continual
	Meta-Learning} (learning to learn from a sequence of tasks) and
\textit{Meta-Continual Learning} (using meta-learning to solve the catastrophic
forgetting problem). Our research aligns with their definition of
Continual Bi-Level Learning, where the optimization process itself is
updated online.

\subsection{Meta-optimization of learning dynamics}
In this framework, the learning process is split into two loops. The inner loop
updates model parameters $\theta$ on the current curriculum stage via standard
gradient descent. The outer loop updates meta-parameters $\phi$ (in our case,
the loss weights) to minimize a validation loss. Algorithms like
La-MAML \citep{gupta2020lamaml} employ this structure to learn
per-parameter learning rates that align gradients across sequential tasks.
\citet{wu2024meta} recently improved upon this with VR-MCL, introducing
variance reduction techniques to stabilize the online Hessian approximation
required for these bi-level updates.

Furthermore, \citet{woo2025meta} extended MCL to Neural Fields
(MCL-NF). They demonstrated that meta-learning could efficiently reconstruct
complex 3D scenes and signals by dynamically modulating the network's capacity,
a process analogous to the resolution-based curriculum proposed in this thesis.

\subsection{The stability-plasticity dilemma in curriculum}
A unique challenge arises when applying meta-learning to curriculum generation:
the stability-plasticity dilemma. In standard continual learning, the goal is
to prevent the forgetting of old tasks (stability) \citep{parisi2019continual}.
In our curriculum context, the dilemma is inverted: the model must be
``plastic'' enough to abandon the smoothed approximation (the easy task) and
``stable'' enough to converge on the hard task.

\citet{banayeeanzade2025theoretical} provide theoretical insights into overparameterized models in such multi-task setups, suggesting that without explicit regularization, the interplay between model capacity and task similarity determines the success of transfer. Pure meta-learning of loss weights can be unstable; without constraints, a meta-learner might exploit the easy smoothed loss to achieve artificially low training error without ever transitioning to the hard target function. This necessitates the monotonic regularization strategies explored in this thesis.

\section{Dynamic reweighting and optimization diagnostics}

\subsection{Learning to reweight examples}
\citet{renLearningReweightExamples2018a} proposed a bilevel procedure
that reweights training examples according to their short-term effect on a clean
validation set. Although their work targets noisy labels rather than curriculum
scheduling, it is directly relevant methodologically: it shows how a validation
objective can control inner-loop updates without manually defining static
weights.

\subsection{Sharpness-aware minimization and flatness probes}
\citet{foretSharpnessAwareMinimizationEfficiently2021} linked
generalization to neighborhood-aware optimization through the
Sharpness-Aware Minimization (SAM) objective. This thesis does not optimize SAM
directly, but uses roughness and sharpness diagnostics inspired by the same
geometry-based perspective. The practical question is whether curriculum gains
coincide with flatter regions of the loss landscape.

\section{Synthesis}

The literature supports the core design of this thesis: continuation methods
motivate easy-to-hard objective transitions
\citep{allgower1990numerical,mobahi2015theoretical,ilersich2025deep},
coarse-to-fine work motivates output-space label curricula
\citep{stretcuCoarsetoFineCurriculumLearning2021}, multi-objective methods
provide the machinery to combine losses \citep{chen2024gradient}, and
meta-learning offers mechanisms for dynamic weighting
\citep{son2024meta,mao2022metaweighting}.

The reviewed work also shows that curriculum learning is not a single mechanism.
Instance-based curricula, such as the teacher-ranked examples of Weinshall et
al., change which examples influence the model first. Objective-based curricula,
such as Gaussian continuation or PINN curriculum regularization, change the
loss itself. Output-space curricula, such as coarse-to-fine classification,
change the label resolution. These interventions can all be described as
``easy-to-hard,'' but they preserve different information and therefore should
not be expected to behave identically.

This is the main gap addressed by the empirical chapters. The continuation
literature gives a theoretical reason why smoothing can help, but the strongest
formal results are available only for restricted function families and do not
provide a computable diagnostic for neural-network losses. Multi-task learning
provides tools for balancing losses, but curriculum learning often requires a
controlled imbalance: the easy loss should dominate early and then yield to the
hard target. Continual-learning work highlights stability and plasticity, but in
curriculum learning the earlier task is scaffolding rather than a permanent
objective. These distinctions motivate the thesis design.

The related work also motivates the control experiments. Teacher-based
curricula show that external models can provide useful ordering or structure,
but the teacher signal is only a proxy for the unknown ideal curriculum.
Coarse-to-fine label hierarchies suggest that class structure can help, but they
also raise the question of whether any label coarsening would help. Sharpness
and flatness work suggests possible optimization mechanisms, but does not prove
that a curriculum gain is caused by flatter minima. The experimental design
therefore combines performance comparisons with random controls, reverse-order
controls, trajectory metrics, and roughness diagnostics.

At the same time, the review exposes clear gaps. Most practical curricula still
use fixed schedules. Standard multi-task balancing aims at simultaneous
equilibrium, not sequential transition. Meta-learning methods can adapt weights,
but without constraints they may overuse easy objectives and delay transfer to
the final task. The empirical part of this thesis is therefore organized around
these unresolved points: when curriculum helps, when it fails, and which design
choices are responsible.

\chapter{Methodology}
\label{chap:methodology}

This chapter defines the common methodological frame used in both empirical
tracks. Dataset-specific details are presented together with the corresponding
results in Chapters~\ref{chap:regression-curriculum} and
\ref{chap:vision-curriculum}. The chapter also separates algorithmic choices
from engineering choices. Infrastructure details such as experiment tracking or
container execution are included only where they affect reproducibility,
provenance, or the feasibility of running comparable experiments.

\section{Research design}

The thesis treats curriculum learning as a controlled modification of the
training objective. A curriculum method is considered useful only if it improves
model quality or optimization behavior relative to a strong non-curriculum
baseline trained under comparable conditions. This requirement is important
because weak baselines can make curriculum effects appear larger than they are.

The empirical work is organized into two tracks. The first track studies
objective-space curricula for regression, where the target function is smoothed
and gradually sharpened. The second track studies output-space curricula for
classification, where coarse labels are replaced by progressively finer labels.
The second track is motivated by the limitations of the first: smoothing can make
optimization easier, but it may also remove information that is needed by the
final task.

The design is deliberately comparative rather than purely constructive. The goal
is not only to show that a curriculum can work, but also to identify when it does
not work. For this reason, negative results, runtime costs, trajectory penalties,
and ablations are treated as part of the evidence rather than as failed
experiments.

\section{Evidence hierarchy}

The experiments are interpreted using an explicit hierarchy of evidence. The
strongest claims require matched baselines, comparable training budgets, and at
least a small amount of seed replication. Single-seed sweeps are used primarily
for screening and for selecting promising regions of the design space. Diagnostic
plots, roughness probes, and best-run examples are treated as explanatory
support, not as standalone evidence.

This distinction matters because several results in the thesis use best test
accuracy along a trajectory. Such a metric is useful for understanding whether a
training procedure ever reaches a better solution, but it is weaker than a
validation-selected test result. Consequently, the thesis separates claims about
\emph{peak quality} from claims about deployable model selection or training
speed.

\section{Curriculum learning as dynamic loss optimization}

Both tracks can be interpreted as dynamic loss optimization. At training step
$t$, the model parameters $\theta$ are updated using a loss
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{L}_t(\theta) = \sum_{k=1}^{K} w_k(t)\mathcal{L}_k(\theta),
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal{L}_k$ are task- or resolution-specific losses and $w_k(t)$ are
schedule weights. In fixed curricula, these weights are predefined. In adaptive
or meta-optimized variants, they depend on training progress or validation
performance.

In the regression track, the component losses correspond to different smoothed
versions of the same target function. In the classification track, the component
losses correspond to different levels of label granularity. This shared view is
useful because it makes the two tracks comparable: both ask whether an easier
objective can guide optimization toward a better solution on the final objective.

The final objective is always the reference point. Let \(\mathcal{L}_\star\)
denote the hard regression target or the fine-label classification loss. A
curriculum is useful only if training through auxiliary losses improves the
model evaluated on \(\mathcal{L}_\star\) or on the corresponding held-out
fine-label metric. Improvement on an intermediate loss is not counted as a
success by itself. This rule prevents an easy but misleading conclusion: a model
can become very good at a smoothed or coarse task while still failing to improve
on the target task.

\section{Regression track}

The regression experiments use synthetic benchmark functions with non-convex
structure. The named functions follow the standard benchmark definitions indexed by
Surjanovic and Bingham (SFU). For each function, the training data contain the original target and a
sequence of Gaussian-smoothed targets. \citep{surjanovic2013benchmark} The main baseline is direct training on
the final, least-smoothed target. Curriculum variants train on a sequence or a
weighted mixture of smoothed objectives before emphasizing the final target.

Formally, for a target function \(f\), the continuation levels approximate
\(f_{\sigma_j} = G_{\sigma_j} * f\), where \(G_{\sigma_j}\) is a Gaussian kernel
and \(\sigma_j\) indexes the smoothing level. The direct baseline minimizes
mean-squared error against \(f_{\sigma_\star}\), the least-smoothed target. A
fixed curriculum instead trains first on larger \(\sigma_j\) values and then
moves toward \(\sigma_\star\). A multi-loss variant optimizes a weighted mixture
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{reg}}(\theta, t)
    = \sum_j w_j(t)\,\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}
      \left(m_\theta(x_i) - f_{\sigma_j}(x_i)\right)^2 .
\end{equation}
The key evaluation rule is that all variants are compared on held-out error for
the hard target, not on the active smoothed target. This is why a curriculum can
look good during early stages but still lose in the final comparison.

Hyperparameter sweeps are part of the methodology rather than an implementation
detail. They reduce the risk of comparing curriculum methods against an
under-tuned single-target baseline. The regression track therefore first
establishes strong baseline configurations and only then evaluates curriculum or
multi-loss variants against them.

\section{Vision track}

The image-classification track uses output-space curricula inspired by
coarse-to-fine label hierarchies. Instead of smoothing the input or target
surface, the method replaces the final label set with coarser label groups early
in training. Later stages introduce finer labels until the original
classification problem is restored.

The model always keeps a fine-label classifier head. During a coarse stage, the
loss marginalizes the predicted probabilities over the active cluster. If
\(C_t(y)\) is the current cluster containing the true fine label \(y\), then the
coarse loss is
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{coarse}}(x,y,t)
    = -\log \sum_{c \in C_t(y)} p_\theta(c \mid x).
\end{equation}
Here, \(p_\theta(c \mid x)\) is the fine-class softmax probability for class
\(c\). The set \(C_t(y)\) contains all fine classes that belong to the active
coarse group of the true label \(y\) at stage \(t\). The summation therefore asks
how much probability the model assigns to the correct coarse group, not whether
it already selected the exact fine class. When \(C_t(y)=\{y\}\), the sum contains
only the true fine class and the expression reduces to ordinary cross-entropy.
This formulation is important because the student can gain fine-label accuracy
before the final stage even though it is not yet trained on singleton labels.
Small clusters are already close proxies for the final task, while broad
clusters provide much weaker fine-label signal.

The hierarchy is learned from model-derived class information, primarily the
geometry of classifier weights after baseline training. The main comparison is
between direct fine-label training and curriculum training with the learned
hierarchy. Additional controls test whether the effect depends on schedule
length, model capacity, dataset difficulty, hierarchy depth, and hierarchy
quality. The random-hierarchy ablation is especially important because it keeps
the amount of early label coarsening fixed while destroying the learned class
grouping.

\section{Baselines and controls}

The main baseline in both tracks is direct training on the final target objective
or final label space. Curriculum variants are compared against this baseline
using matched architectures, training budgets, data splits, and, where possible,
hyperparameter search. The experiments also include several controls:

\begin{itemize}
    \item model-capacity comparisons, especially in the CIFAR-100 vision track,
    \item fixed versus adaptive curriculum schedules,
    \item shallow versus deeper hierarchy usage,
    \item cross-dataset checks on easier and harder datasets,
    \item random hierarchy controls with matched cluster sizes.
\end{itemize}

These controls are necessary because a curriculum can help for several different
reasons: it may provide meaningful structure, reduce early-task complexity,
regularize optimization, or simply delay hard-label training. The methodology is
therefore designed to separate these explanations where possible.

A consequence of this design is that some comparisons are deliberately
conservative. For example, the teacher hierarchy is not only compared with the
baseline; it is compared with self-derived hierarchies, random hierarchies, and
the reverse-order hierarchy. Similarly, adaptive schedules are not compared only
with direct training; they are compared with the best fixed schedules available
under the same training budget. These conservative comparisons make the final
claims narrower, but they also make them more credible.

\section{Evaluation protocol}

The primary metrics are final validation or test performance, best achieved
performance during training, and trajectory-based diagnostics such as area under
the learning curve. The motivation for AUC is convergence speed: it summarizes
how good the model is throughout training, not only at the best checkpoint. When
this thesis writes AUC, it means a normalized area under a learning trajectory,
computed with the trapezoidal rule over epochs; it does \emph{not} mean ROC AUC.
For the regression track, the main quality measure is prediction error on
held-out samples, and trajectory AUC is computed over the hard-target validation
loss, where lower is better. For the classification track, the main quality
measure is top-1 accuracy, and trajectory AUC is computed over test accuracy,
where higher is better. Additional classification metrics, including macro-F1,
top-5 accuracy, calibration error, and hierarchy-aware scores, are used where
they clarify whether accuracy gains reflect broader improvements.

Best achieved test performance is useful for understanding training dynamics,
but it is retrospective. It should not be confused with a deployment protocol in
which the epoch is selected using validation data and the test set is evaluated
once. For this reason, the discussion distinguishes peak-accuracy evidence from
trajectory evidence. A curriculum that reaches a higher peak but has lower AUC is
interpreted as improving the best solution found, not as accelerating training.

Roughness, Hessian, and sharpness probes are treated as diagnostics rather than
as proof of mechanism. They can support or weaken a flattening interpretation,
but they do not by themselves establish that curriculum causes the optimizer to
find flatter minima.

\section{Experiment tracking and reproducibility}

The implementation uses scripted experiment entry points, explicit configuration
files or command-line arguments, fixed random seeds where possible, and stored
run metadata. Optuna \citep{akiba2019optuna} is used for several
hyperparameter and schedule searches. The regression track uses MLflow
\citep{zaharia2018accelerating} to record parameters, metrics, artifacts, and
run structure. The vision track uses Weights \& Biases \citep{biewald2020experiment}
for the same purpose; W\&B is the source of truth for the later CIFAR-100
\citep{krizhevsky2009learning}, Fashion-MNIST \citep{xiao2017fashion}, CIFAR-10
\citep{krizhevsky2009learning}, and Tiny ImageNet \citep{tinyimagenet,deng2009imagenet}
analyses. Tables and figures in the appendices are derived from exported
tracking data rather than from manually copied console logs.

The large vision experiments were executed in a containerized environment. The
Docker image included the PyTorch \citep{paszke2019pytorch} and CUDA-related
dependencies required by the training code, was versioned and pushed to Docker
Hub, and was then used on Runpod, a cloud service for renting GPU instances, to
run consecutive experiment batches. This is not an algorithmic contribution, and
it is not used as evidence that the method works. It is reported because it
makes the compute environment and dependency set more stable across long-running
experiments, which is important for reproducibility and for interpreting failed
or repeated runs.

Detailed run reports are kept in the appendices so that the thesis preserves the
connection between the claims and the executed experiments without turning the
main chapters into run logs. Appendix~\ref{app:run-provenance}, especially
Table~\ref{tab:run-provenance}, lists the local MLflow experiments, W\&B
exports, analysis folders, and filtering rules behind the main empirical
figures and tables. Supporting exports are cited only when they are converted
into a thesis figure, table, or compact protocol summary.


\chapter{Objective-space curriculum for regression tasks}
\label{chap:regression-curriculum}

This chapter presents the original empirical track of the thesis: curriculum
learning through objective-space continuation on synthetic non-convex regression
tasks. Here, \emph{regression task} means supervised function approximation: the
model receives two coordinates and predicts a scalar value of a known benchmark
surface. The goal was to test whether Gaussian-inspired smoothing and dynamic
multi-loss schedules make difficult target functions easier to learn. The main
question for this track is \hyperref[rq:continuation]{RQ1}: whether continuation
still helps after the direct single-target baseline has been tuned carefully.

\section{Research question and experimental logic}

The regression track evaluates a narrow hypothesis: if a target surface is hard
because of non-convex local structure, then training first on smoother versions
of the same surface may guide the optimizer toward better final solutions. This
hypothesis is tested against a direct hard-target baseline, not against an
untuned default model. The track therefore proceeds in three steps:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item tune model and optimizer hyperparameters for each benchmark function,
    \item compare direct single-target training with fixed continuation
          schedules under matched conditions,
    \item test whether adaptive multi-loss weighting can improve on fixed or
          single-target training in a difficult representative case.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Benchmark functions and continuation targets}

The experiments use eight two-dimensional synthetic regression functions:
\texttt{ackley}, \texttt{levy}, \texttt{bukin}, \texttt{eggholder},
\texttt{franke}, \texttt{peaks}, \texttt{friedman1\_2d}, and
\texttt{friedman2\_2d}. Figure~\ref{fig:main-regression-functions} shows the
surfaces. The set deliberately mixes smooth surfaces, bowl-like optimization
benchmarks, and highly rugged targets. For each function, data are generated
from the analytical target and stored as train and test parquet files. The
continuation variants use multiple target columns corresponding to different
Gaussian smoothing levels. The hardest level is the original or least-smoothed
target and is the metric used for final comparison.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.96\textwidth]{figures/regression_benchmark_functions.png}
    \caption{Synthetic regression-task surfaces used in the continuation track.
    Color scales are clipped per panel for readability, so the figure is meant
    to show qualitative geometry rather than compare absolute function values.}
    \label{fig:main-regression-functions}
\end{figure}

The later v3 setting introduced a stricter protocol: 20,000 generated samples
per function, a 10,000/10,000 train--test split, an internal 80/20
train--validation split inside the training file, additive Gaussian label noise,
three smoothing levels, and an explicit parameter-to-training-samples
constraint. These details are kept in Appendix~\ref{app:sweep-pack-v3-setup},
and Table~\ref{tab:app-regression-version-summary} summarizes how the v1, v2,
and v3 regression experiments differ. The stricter setting checks whether
earlier conclusions were artifacts of small data or oversized models.

\section{Baseline tuning protocol}

Before comparing curricula, the first sweep pack optimized architecture and
training hyperparameters separately for each function. The search covered MLP,
SIREN \citep{sitzmann2020implicit}, and Fourier-feature networks
\citep{tancik2020fourier} and optimized validation MSE. SIREN and Fourier
features were included because coordinate-based regression tasks are vulnerable
to spectral bias: standard MLPs tend to learn low-frequency variation before
high-frequency detail \citep{rahaman2019spectral}. This bias is useful as an
implicit smoothness prior, but it can be harmful for low-dimensional coordinate
signals in graphics, vision, and surface-fitting tasks where sharp oscillations
or fine spatial detail matter. SIREN uses sinusoidal activations, while Fourier
features encode coordinates with sinusoidal positional features before the MLP;
both give the network easier access to high-frequency structure.

The result was not a single universal model family: MLPs were strongest overall,
Fourier features were best on \texttt{eggholder}, and SIREN was best on
\texttt{peaks}. This justified using per-function best configurations in the
subsequent curriculum comparison instead of imposing one manual architecture on
all functions.

\section{Fixed continuation versus direct training}

The main fixed-schedule comparison used a factorial design with two methods: a
\emph{direct hard-target baseline} and a fixed continuation curriculum. In the
experiment logs this direct baseline is named \texttt{single}, because it trains
on a single-target column: the hardest, least-smoothed version of the function.
The design used two budget regimes. The first was an epoch-matched comparison.
The second is named \texttt{equal\_time} in the logs, but more precisely it is a
time-capped comparison: both methods used the same wall-clock timeout cap and
epoch scale, while the realized runtimes were recorded separately and were not
forced to be equal. The matrix covered eight functions and three seeds, for 96
top-level completed runs. Table~\ref{tab:main-regression-counts} summarizes the
result in the main text; the full report is given in
Appendix~\ref{app:curr-vs-single-v1}.

Taken together, the MLflow evidence suggests three conclusions. First,
continuation is not simply ``more training'': it follows a visibly different path
through function space, which is why the final surfaces can differ even when the
same architecture and data are used. Second, the early smoothed stages are useful
only if their low-frequency structure points toward a good solution for the hard
target. This seems plausible for \texttt{bukin}, \texttt{franke}, and parts of
\texttt{friedman1\_2d}; it is much weaker for \texttt{ackley}, \texttt{peaks},
and especially \texttt{eggholder}, where the smoothed target can hide the local
structure that later dominates the hard loss. Third, convergence speed and final
quality are different claims. The AUC and trajectory plots show that the direct
baseline usually becomes competent on the hard target earlier, while the
curriculum sometimes recovers later and occasionally reaches a better endpoint.
The continuation track should therefore be read as evidence for conditional path
shaping, not as evidence for a general optimization speedup.

Sensitivity to optimization hyperparameters is the main practical weakness of
this meta-weighting implementation. Figure~\ref{fig:main-meta-weighting-sensitivity}
shows that validation loss varies strongly with model learning rate and label
noise. This is consistent with the bilevel nature of the method: the outer
update relies on short-horizon validation feedback, so noisy or poorly scaled
inner updates can produce unstable meta-gradients. A more robust implementation
would likely need longer unrolls, stronger regularization on the weight
trajectory, or a schedule parameterization that prevents abrupt changes.

The result narrows the claim: adaptive multi-loss continuation is feasible and

\section{Track-level conclusion}

The regression track answers \hyperref[rq:continuation]{RQ1} cautiously. Objective-space continuation can
improve final quality on some non-convex surfaces, but the effect is not general
and comes with runtime cost. The strongest methodological lesson is that
curriculum claims are fragile unless the direct baseline is strong. This finding
motivated the second track: if simple smoothing is not reliably beneficial, a
more structured curriculum may be needed, where intermediate tasks preserve
semantic information rather than merely removing high-frequency detail.


\chapter{Output-space hierarchical curriculum for vision}
\label{chap:vision-curriculum}

This chapter presents the second empirical track of the thesis. It moves from
smoothing regression targets to coarse-to-fine supervision in image
classification. The goal is to test whether curriculum learning is more useful
when intermediate tasks preserve semantic structure instead of only reducing
objective roughness. This chapter addresses \hyperref[rq:vision]{RQ2} and
\hyperref[rq:adaptive]{RQ3} most directly.

\section{Research question and experimental logic}

The vision track tests a different form of easy-to-hard training. Instead of
changing the input data or smoothing a regression target, the model first learns
a coarser output space and later returns to the original fine-label problem. The
central hypothesis is that coarse labels can provide useful intermediate
structure when the final label space is large enough and the model has limited
capacity.

This track was designed as a sequence of increasingly strict controls:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item a CIFAR-100 model-size screening sweep,
    \item multi-seed fixed and adaptive schedule follow-ups,
    \item dataset controls on CIFAR-10, Fashion-MNIST, and Tiny ImageNet,
    \item hierarchy inspection and a matched random-hierarchy ablation,
    \item teacher-derived hierarchy, random hierarchy, and reverse-order
          hierarchy controls.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Coarse-to-fine training method}

The implementation follows the continuous coarse-to-fine formulation of \citet{stretcuCoarsetoFineCurriculumLearning2021}: the model keeps a
fine-class classifier head, but the training loss is evaluated after
marginalizing fine-label probabilities into the active coarse clusters. The
hierarchy is generated from model-derived class similarity, primarily the
classifier-weight geometry of a baseline model. Thus, the hierarchy is not a
manual taxonomy; it is a model-specific estimate of which classes are close in
the learned representation.

A fixed curriculum allocates a predetermined number of epochs to coarse labels
before fine-label training. The adaptive plateau curriculum starts at a coarse
level and advances when fine-label validation accuracy stops improving after a
minimum number of epochs or when a maximum stage length is reached. This rule is
intended to reduce manual curriculum-length tuning, but the experiments below
show that it can also stay too long in coarse stages.

\section{CIFAR-100 model-size study}

The first screening study used 80 completed CIFAR-100 runs
\citep{krizhevsky2009learning}: one baseline and five curriculum lengths for
each model configuration, mostly with seed 42. The training budget was 100
epochs with Adam \citep{kingma2015adam}, learning rate 0.001, and curriculum
lengths 5, 10, 20, 30, and 40. The capacity question is practically motivated:
small networks are attractive for resource-constrained or edge deployment,
where models such as MobileNets trade accuracy for efficiency
\citep{howard2017mobilenets}. A curriculum that helps a small student learn a
better representation is related in spirit to teacher--student and knowledge
distillation arguments \citep{hinton2015distilling}, although the method here
does not train on teacher logits. It changes the output granularity of the
supervised task instead. The full table is in
Appendix~\ref{app:cifar100-model-size-curriculum}; Table~\ref{tab:main-cifar100-capacity}
keeps the core pattern in the main text.

\section{Fixed schedules, adaptive schedules, and hierarchy depth}

The follow-up repeated important CIFAR-100 cases over seeds 42, 43, and 44 and
added adaptive plateau schedules. The adaptive variants differ in hierarchy
coverage. ``Adaptive all levels'' may traverse every generated non-singleton
coarse level before fine-label training. ``Adaptive first level'' uses only the
first generated non-singleton level---the broadest usable partition after
removing the trivial root cluster---and then switches to fine labels. This tests
whether a short coarse warm-up is enough, rather than forcing the scheduler to
visit every available hierarchy depth. Table~\ref{tab:main-cifar100-policy}
summarizes the central comparison from Appendix~\ref{app:roughness-followup-curriculum}.

The best fixed schedules tested here remain stronger than this particular

still useful as a schedule-selection heuristic, but it often allocates too many
epochs to coarse levels, especially for larger CNNs whose best fixed schedule is
very short. The first-level-only adaptive variant is consistently better than
using all generated levels where both were tested, showing that hierarchy depth
is a first-order design choice rather than a harmless implementation detail.

therefore reduces manual schedule selection only partially: it still encodes a
strong assumption about how long coarse training should continue before the
fine-label objective takes over.

\section{Dataset controls}

The same curriculum idea was tested on easier or different datasets. CIFAR-10
\citep{krizhevsky2009learning} shows small positive gains for weak CNNs,
Fashion-MNIST \citep{xiao2017fashion} is essentially neutral or negative, and
Tiny ImageNet \citep{tinyimagenet,deng2009imagenet} is mixed: small Adam-trained
models were negative, while a stronger ResNet-18 \citep{he2016deep} with
SGD/cosine \citep{loshchilov2017sgdr} achieved a modest positive gain. The
important conclusion is that weak model capacity alone is not sufficient. The
dataset must also contain class structure that is useful at coarse granularity.

Figure~\ref{fig:main-dataset-controls} summarizes the follow-up datasets. The
contrast between CIFAR-100 and Fashion-MNIST is especially informative. Both can
be trained with small CNNs, but CIFAR-100 has a larger and more structured label
space where coarse groupings can provide useful intermediate supervision.
Fashion-MNIST has only ten classes and much less need for a coarse-to-fine
output curriculum, so the same mechanism has little room to help.

\begin{figure}[htbp]

architecture, and schedule are better matched to the dataset. This reinforces
the thesis-level pattern: curriculum benefits are conditional interactions, not
standalone properties of a dataset.

\section{Teacher-derived and reverse-order hierarchy controls}

The final follow-up tested whether the hierarchy source itself was the main
limitation. A ResNet-18 initialized from ImageNet and fine-tuned on CIFAR-100
reached 77.65\%--78.14\% best test accuracy in short transfer-learning probes,
substantially stronger than the earlier from-scratch ResNet-18 baseline in this
thesis. This number is a test-accuracy summary of the teacher; the later use of
``validation'' refers to the held-out split from which teacher embeddings were
extracted, not to a separate validation-accuracy comparison. Those embeddings
were used to build a teacher-derived class hierarchy for the weak
\texttt{cnn\_w0.5} student.

This experiment also sharpened the interpretation of the controls. The random
hierarchy keeps the tree shape and cluster sizes but permutes the class
assignments, so it tests whether arbitrary label bundling is useful. The
reverse-order teacher condition uses the same teacher hierarchy, but visits the
non-singleton levels from finer to coarser before the final fine-label phase. It
is not training on wrong labels and it is not a pure negative control, because
the class bundles are still structured.

The result separates two effects. First, coarse-label warm-up is useful even
when the grouping is random: random hierarchies improve peak accuracy by almost
one percentage point. Second, structure still matters: self-derived,
teacher-derived, and reverse-order teacher curricula all exceed the random mean.
The teacher hierarchy does not clearly improve peak accuracy over the
self-derived hierarchy, which suggests that hierarchy source quality is not the
only bottleneck. Schedule length, marginalised coarse-label loss, and weak
student capacity also constrain the final effect.

\section{Track-level conclusion}

The vision track answers \hyperref[rq:vision]{RQ2} positively but conditionally. Coarse-to-fine
curriculum learning can improve peak accuracy for lower-capacity models on
structured many-class datasets, especially CIFAR-100. The effect weakens on
larger models, easier datasets, and poorly matched hierarchies. The random and
teacher-control results add an important caveat: part of the gain comes from the
coarse-label training protocol itself, while meaningful class structure adds a
smaller but consistent extra benefit. For \hyperref[rq:adaptive]{RQ3}, the
answer is narrower: the tested adaptive plateau rule reduces manual tuning in
principle, but in these experiments it does not outperform well-chosen fixed
schedules.

\section{Interpretation across both tracks}

Two conclusions are robust at this stage. First, curriculum gains are real but
conditional. Second, schedule and hierarchy design matter at least as much as
adding more curriculum stages.

The regression track shows that smoothing the objective can make the early task
easier, but this does not automatically translate into a better final model once
the single-target baseline is well tuned. The vision track suggests a more
promising condition: curricula are more useful when intermediate tasks preserve
meaningful structure, such as class hierarchy, while still reducing difficulty.
This difference motivates the central interpretation of the thesis: curriculum
learning is not beneficial merely because training starts with an easier task;
it is beneficial when the easier task provides information that transfers to the
final task.

\section{Why objective-space and output-space curricula behave differently}

The two tracks implement the same high-level idea---begin with an easier
objective and later optimize the true target---but they differ in what is
preserved by the easier objective. In Gaussian continuation, smoothing removes
high-frequency structure from the target function. This can be helpful if the
removed structure is mostly distracting local roughness, but harmful if the
smoothed objective changes the basins that matter for the final target. The
continuation path is therefore sensitive to the geometry of each benchmark
function. This explains why the same schedule can help \texttt{bukin} and
\texttt{levy} but hurt \texttt{eggholder} or \texttt{peaks}.

In the output-space vision track, the intermediate objective does not smooth the
input or remove images. It changes the label granularity while keeping the
fine-class classifier head. This means that the student still sees the full
image distribution and learns features from the same examples, but the early
loss asks it to separate groups of classes rather than all fine labels at once.
If the groups are meaningful, the early task can teach representations that are
still useful for the final task. This makes the vision curriculum less dependent
on an analytic continuation path and more dependent on hierarchy quality,
schedule length, and model capacity.

This comparison also highlights why strong baselines are essential in curriculum
studies. A curriculum can look better simply because the direct baseline was
under-tuned or because the curriculum run effectively receives more
optimization. For that reason, this thesis treats baseline strength and budget
matching as part of the method, not as implementation detail. In several
regression settings, improving the direct baseline reduced or eliminated
apparent curriculum gains.

\section{Peak accuracy versus trajectory performance}

A repeated pattern in the vision experiments is that curriculum improves the
best checkpoint but lowers trajectory AUC. This is not a contradiction. During
the coarse phase, the model is optimized for a marginalized cluster loss, so the
100-way fine-label metric is not the direct training target. The baseline, by
contrast, trains on the fine labels from the first epoch. It is therefore
expected that the baseline dominates early fine-label accuracy.

The meaningful question is what happens after the switch. In the positive
CIFAR-100 cases, the curriculum models recover quickly and can reach a higher
peak than the baseline. This supports a warm-up or regularization
interpretation: the coarse phase changes the representation or optimization
state from which fine-label training begins. However, it also means that the
method should not be advertised as faster training. It is better described as a
procedure that can improve the best solution reached within a fixed budget while
sacrificing early fine-label performance.

This distinction matters for evaluation. If the application requires the best
possible final checkpoint and validation-based model selection is available,
peak accuracy is relevant. If the application values anytime performance or
short training budgets, trajectory AUC and early milestones are more important.
The experiments in this thesis mostly support the first use case, not the
second.

\section{Why random coarsening helps}

The random-hierarchy result is one of the most important controls in the thesis.
It shows that part of the benefit does not come from semantic hierarchy quality
at all. Even arbitrary label bundles reduce the effective number of decision
regions early in training and temporarily relax the need to separate all fine
classes. This can act like an output bottleneck or a regularizer. The student
learns features while being protected from the full 100-way discrimination
problem, then later fine-tunes the same classifier head on exact labels.

This does not make hierarchy structure irrelevant. Random coarsening is
consistently below the learned and teacher-derived hierarchies. The correct
interpretation is additive rather than exclusive: there is a general
coarse-label warm-up effect, and meaningful grouping adds an extra benefit. This
is why the random control is stronger than a no-curriculum baseline but weaker
than structured curricula.

The reverse-order teacher control reinforces the same point. It violates the
intended easy-to-hard order, but it still uses structured class bundles. It also
starts from the finest non-singleton hierarchy level, which is already close to
the final 100-way task. Its positive result therefore should not be treated as a
failure of curriculum theory. It shows that temporal order and class grouping
are partially separable factors: easy-to-hard order helps, but structured
bundling itself can remain useful even in the wrong order.

\section{Why the teacher hierarchy did not dominate the self hierarchy}

The transfer teacher was much stronger than the weak CNN student, and its
hierarchy was qualitatively more plausible than the earlier weak self-derived
hierarchies. Nevertheless, the teacher-derived curriculum did not clearly
outperform the self-derived hierarchy in peak accuracy. This is a useful negative result.
It suggests that hierarchy quality is not the only limiting factor.

Several bottlenecks remain. First, the schedule is fixed: the teacher hierarchy
may require a different number of coarse epochs than the self-derived hierarchy.
Second, the loss marginalizes probability mass over clusters, which may not be
the best way to transfer teacher information. Third, the student model is weak;
a cleaner hierarchy cannot fully compensate for limited representation capacity.
Fourth, a teacher representation learned for high classification accuracy may
capture image-distribution similarities that are useful but not perfectly aligned
with the student's optimization path.

The teacher result therefore changes the thesis claim from ``better hierarchy
solves curriculum learning'' to a more precise statement: hierarchy quality
matters, but it interacts with schedule, loss formulation, and student capacity.
This is a stronger and more defensible conclusion.

\section{Practical recommendations from the experiments}

The empirical results suggest several practical rules for future curriculum
experiments:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item Always compare against a tuned direct baseline. Curriculum gains are
          not meaningful if the baseline is weak.
    \item Report both peak performance and trajectory metrics. A curriculum may
          improve the best checkpoint while delaying learning for many epochs.
    \item Include random or mismatched hierarchy controls when using label
          hierarchies. Otherwise, improvements can be mistaken for semantic
          structure effects when they partly come from label coarsening itself.
    \item Treat adaptive schedules as hypotheses, not automatic improvements.
          The plateau rule used here often stayed too long in coarse stages.
    \item Inspect the hierarchy qualitatively and quantitatively. A hierarchy
          need not match human taxonomy exactly, but it should be more coherent
          than a random partition under the representation used by the model.
\end{enumerate}

These recommendations are methodological rather than algorithmic contributions,
but they are central to the thesis. Many curriculum-learning claims can look
strong under a narrow comparison and weaken once these controls are added.

\section{Answers to the research questions}

\paragraph{\hyperref[rq:continuation]{RQ1}: objective-space continuation.}
Gaussian-inspired continuation helps on selected regression functions, but it is
not reliable after strong baseline tuning. The direct hard-target baseline has
lower loss on several functions and consistently lower runtime. The answer is therefore
conditional rather than affirmative.

\paragraph{\hyperref[rq:vision]{RQ2}: output-space hierarchy.}
Coarse-to-fine supervision is more promising in structured many-class vision
settings. The strongest evidence is CIFAR-100 with lower-capacity models, where
peak accuracy improves and learned hierarchies outperform matched random
hierarchies. The teacher-control follow-up refines this claim: arbitrary
coarsening already helps, but structured self-derived and teacher-derived
hierarchies help more. The effect weakens for larger models and easier datasets.

\paragraph{\hyperref[rq:adaptive]{RQ3}: adaptive scheduling.}
The current adaptive methods do not yet match the best fixed schedules tested in
this thesis. Meta-weighting in the regression track is feasible but not better on
average, and adaptive plateau scheduling in the vision track often stays too long
in coarse stages. Adaptive scheduling remains a direction for future work rather
than a completed solution.

\paragraph{\hyperref[rq:diagnostics]{RQ4}: optimization mechanism.}
The diagnostic evidence is inconclusive. AUC often decreases, so curriculum does
not generally accelerate training. Roughness and sharpness probes sometimes
support a flatter-minima interpretation, especially for selected ResNet cases,
but the pattern is not consistent enough to claim a general mechanism.

\section{Limitations and threats to validity}

The first limitation is statistical power. Several important follow-ups use
three seeds, which is enough to detect large and consistent effects but not
enough for strong significance claims. Some screening runs are single-seed by
design. They are useful for mapping the design space, but they should not be
read as final estimates of effect size. This is why the thesis emphasizes sign
patterns, matched comparisons, and consistency across controls rather than
formal hypothesis testing.

The second limitation is model-selection protocol. Many headline tables report
best test accuracy along the trajectory. This is appropriate for diagnosing
whether a training procedure ever reaches a better solution, but it is not a
strict deployment protocol. A final benchmark should select checkpoints using a
validation set and evaluate the test set once. The current evidence is therefore
strongest as an optimization and training-dynamics study, not as a claim that a
particular validation-selected model would always deploy better.

The third limitation is schedule search. Fixed schedules were selected from a
small set of curriculum lengths, and the adaptive plateau rule uses manually
chosen patience and stage-length parameters. The negative adaptive result should
therefore be interpreted as a limitation of this adaptive rule, not as a proof
that adaptive curriculum scheduling cannot work. Similarly, the meta-weighting
experiments use a one-step unroll for computational reasons. Longer unrolls or
more constrained schedule parameterizations could produce different behavior.

The fourth limitation is hierarchy construction. The self-derived hierarchy uses
classifier-weight geometry, while the teacher hierarchy uses embeddings from a
transfer model. Both are reasonable proxies for class similarity, but neither is
an oracle hierarchy. The random controls show that grouping matters, but they do
not identify the optimal grouping. The teacher-control result further shows that
a stronger teacher does not automatically create a better student curriculum.
This means that hierarchy quality, schedule design, loss formulation, and
student capacity should be studied jointly.

The fifth limitation concerns mechanism claims. Roughness, Hessian, and
sharpness probes are correlational diagnostics. They can show that a curriculum
run ends in a region with different local geometry, but they do not prove that
this geometry caused the accuracy gain. The safest claim is that flatter-minima
interpretations are plausible in selected cases and unsupported in others.

Finally, the experiments are restricted to two broad domains: synthetic
regression surfaces and image classification. The regression functions are useful
because they expose controlled non-convex geometry, but they are not real-world
scientific datasets. The vision experiments are broader, but still mostly focus
on CIFAR-100 and CNN-style students. The thesis therefore provides a controlled
empirical foundation, not a universal benchmark of curriculum learning.

Given these limits, the current claims are:

\begin{quote}
Curriculum learning is not a universal improvement strategy. It is most useful
in selected regimes where model capacity, hierarchy quality, and schedule
choices align with dataset structure.
\end{quote}

\chapter{Conclusions}
\label{chap:conclusions}

This thesis investigated dynamic curriculum learning in two complementary forms:
objective-space continuation for regression and output-space hierarchical
curricula for image classification. The central motivation was to move beyond
static easy-to-hard schedules and evaluate when adaptive or hierarchy-based
curricula provide measurable benefits.

The results support a nuanced conclusion. Curriculum effects are reproducible in
selected settings, but they are not universal. In the regression track,
objective-space continuation improves selected functions but does not reliably
outperform tuned single-target training. In the vision track, hierarchical curricula
are more promising, especially for lower-capacity CIFAR-100 models, but the gain
still depends on dataset structure, hierarchy quality, and schedule length.
Where these conditions are not met, curriculum often increases training cost
without improving the overall trajectory.

The numerical anchors make this conclusion concrete. In the fixed regression
benchmark, continuation achieved lower final loss on 5 of 8 functions under the
epoch-matched budget and 3 of 8 under the time-capped budget, while direct
hard-target training had lower runtime on all 8 functions in both regimes. In the CIFAR-100 model-size
screening, the largest gains were in the small-CNN regime, for example +3.18 pp
for \texttt{cnn\_w0.5} and +3.62 pp for \texttt{cnn\_w1}. In the hierarchy-control
suite, random coarsening itself improved the weak-CNN baseline by about +0.97 pp,
but structured self-derived and teacher-derived hierarchies reached about +2.3 pp.
Thus, the final claim is neither uniformly positive nor negative: curriculum
helps when the intermediate objective is aligned with the final task and when the
schedule does not overstay the easier stage.

The thesis also clarifies what can and cannot be claimed mechanistically.
Roughness and sharpness diagnostics are informative, but current evidence does
not justify a general statement that curriculum consistently finds flatter
minima. The safer interpretation is empirical: curriculum can improve the best
solution reached by training in some regimes, even when early-trajectory metrics
remain worse.

Methodologically, the work contributes a reproducible experimental workflow and
closes a key control gap through random, teacher-derived, and reverse-order
hierarchy controls. In the main CIFAR-100 weak-CNN setting, learned and teacher
hierarchies outperform matched random hierarchies, which supports the claim that
hierarchy quality carries useful information beyond simple early-stage label
reduction. At the same time, random coarsening also improves over baseline, so
the final interpretation must separate a general coarse-label warm-up effect
from the additional benefit of meaningful class structure. For strict
model-selection claims, checkpoints should be selected on validation data and
the test set should be used once for final reporting.

\section{Future work}

The next research step is consolidation rather than broad expansion. A useful
extension is validation-selected evaluation for all major runs. The current best
checkpoint analysis is valuable for understanding optimization trajectories; it
is not ``wrong,'' but it answers a diagnostic question rather than a strict
model-selection question. A stricter benchmark would select epochs using
validation data and then report one held-out test result.

The second priority is stronger adaptive scheduling. The plateau rule used here
is intentionally simple, but the results show that simplicity is not enough. A
better adaptive method should estimate whether the coarse stage is still
improving the future fine-label objective, not merely whether validation
accuracy has plateaued. Possible directions include validation-gradient signals,
small lookahead updates, Bayesian stopping rules for stage transitions, or
schedule parameterizations that learn only a few monotone transition points.

The third priority is hierarchy optimization. The teacher probe shows that a
stronger representation can produce a plausible hierarchy, but the resulting
student curriculum does not clearly outperform the self-derived hierarchy. Future work
should therefore optimize the hierarchy and schedule together. For example, one
could compare embedding hierarchies from multiple teachers, use validation loss
to select hierarchy depth, or learn soft class groupings instead of hard
clusters. The random-hierarchy result also suggests that the control set should
be retained: every new hierarchy method should be compared against random
coarsening with the same shape.

The fourth priority is mechanism testing. Roughness and sharpness diagnostics
should be paired with interventions that can actually test causality. For
example, if curriculum is hypothesized to help by finding flatter minima, then
combining it with sharpness-aware optimization or explicitly matching final
sharpness across methods would provide a stronger test. Similarly, if random
coarsening acts as an output bottleneck, then it should be compared with other
regularizers that reduce early classification pressure.

Finally, the two tracks could be integrated more tightly. The regression track
uses explicit objective smoothing but lacks semantic structure; the vision track
uses structured label coarsening but a relatively simple schedule. A natural
next step is to design curricula that combine both ideas: learned intermediate
objectives whose difficulty and structure are adjusted online according to
validation performance. Such methods would be closer to the original goal of
dynamic multi-loss curriculum learning.

Taken together, the thesis provides a credible first-stage answer: dynamic
curriculum learning is promising, but its benefit is conditional and must be
established with careful controls, not assumed by default.
