Training deep neural networks is a complex process, especially because their
loss landscapes are often non-convex and filled with many local minima.
Traditional training methods usually treat data and objectives as static, which
can overlook important aspects of the learning process. The idea of curriculum
learning, introduced by Elman \cite{elman1993learning} and formalized by Bengio
et al. \cite{bengio2009curriculum}, suggests that models can achieve better
results when they are exposed to tasks in order of increasing difficulty. This
concept is closely related to continuation methods from optimization theory,
which use smoothing techniques to make the loss landscape easier to navigate at
first, and then gradually restore its complexity \cite{allgower1990numerical}.
Recent research, such as \cite{ilersich2025deep}, has shown that using Gaussian
kernels to smooth the objective function can help optimizers avoid poor local
minima and improve overall performance.

One of the main challenges in these approaches is the use of fixed, manually
chosen schedules for adjusting the level of smoothing. These schedules often do
not respond to the actual progress of the model during training
\cite{wang2021survey}. The aim of this thesis is to develop a method that
automates this process using data-driven techniques. The curriculum is
generated as a continual bi-level learning problem \cite{son2024meta}, where
meta-learning is used to adjust the weights of different loss functions
dynamically. Unlike standard multi-task learning, which tries to balance tasks
equally \cite{chen2024gradient}, this approach focuses on guiding the model
from easier, smoothed objectives to more difficult, sharp ones as training
progresses.

This research involves designing and testing a meta-learning algorithm that can
automatically adjust the weights of smoothed loss functions. It is important to
prevent the model from focusing only on the easiest objectives, which can
happen if the meta-learning process is unconstrained. To address this, a
monotonic gradient regularizer is introduced, ensuring that the model
consistently moves from easy to hard tasks. The effectiveness of this approach
is evaluated on regression problems with non-convex loss landscapes, using
Gaussian smoothing kernels. This work aims to connect theoretical ideas from
continuation methods with practical meta-optimization strategies
\cite{gupta2025efficient, mao2022metaweighting}.

\section{Curriculum Learning and Numerical Continuation}
\label{sec:curriculum-continuation}

The idea that models benefit from learning tasks in order of increasing
difficulty was formalized by Bengio et al. \cite{bengio2009curriculum}, drawing
on earlier work by Elman \cite{elman1993learning}. Elman showed that neural
networks with limited capacity could learn complex structures if they started
with simpler tasks. These early constraints are not just shortcuts, but
important for guiding the learning process.

\subsection{Objective-Based Curriculum}
Recent research divides curriculum learning into two main types: instance-based
and objective-based \cite{soviany2022curriculum}. Instance-based methods sort
data by features like noise or complexity, while objective-based approaches
change the definition of the task itself. The latter is closely linked to
numerical continuation methods \cite{allgower1990numerical}, which are used in
global optimization. In these methods, training starts with a smoothed, convex
version of the objective function, making it easier to find a good solution. As
training continues, the smoothing is gradually reduced, allowing the model to
tackle more complex aspects of the problem.

\subsection{Gaussian Continuation}
One practical way to apply continuation methods in deep learning is through
Gaussian continuation. Mobahi \cite{mobahi2015theoretical} showed that
convolving the objective function with Gaussian kernels is an effective way to
smooth the loss landscape, making it easier for optimizers to find global
minima. Later work by Ilersich et al. \cite{ilersich2025deep} provided
mathematical evidence that this approach can transform non-convex landscapes
into ones that are much easier to optimize. Empirical studies, such as
\cite{sinha2020curriculum}, have used Gaussian smoothing to filter out noise
during training, and \cite{gupta2025efficient} demonstrated that using
intermediate checkpoints as smoothed objectives can guide models through a
gradual learning process.

Recent robust curriculum methods also operate directly at the loss level.
SuperLoss appends a generic adaptive term to existing objectives and
automatically downweights high-loss samples, which improves robustness in noisy
settings without requiring task-specific training pipelines
\cite{castells2020superloss}. At the evaluation level, CurBench established a
unified benchmark and toolkit for curriculum learning across vision, language,
and graph domains, making cross-method comparisons more reproducible
\cite{zhou2024curbench}.

Despite these advances, most existing methods rely on fixed schedules for
adjusting the level of smoothing or regularization. These schedules are often
chosen manually and do not adapt to the model's progress \cite{wang2021survey}.
This limitation motivates the development of automated, data-driven scheduling
methods, which is a central focus of this thesis.

\section{Multi-Task and Multi-Objective Optimization}

To move between objectives of different difficulty, the problem can be set up
as a multi-task learning scenario. Here, the total loss is a weighted sum of
several component losses. However, curriculum learning and multi-task learning
have different goals when it comes to how these tasks interact.

\subsection{Gradient Conflict and Balancing}
In multi-task learning, the usual goal is to balance progress across all tasks.
One common problem is negative transfer, where learning one task can interfere
with another. Methods like GradNorm \cite{chen2018gradnorm} adjust task weights
to keep gradient magnitudes similar, while PCGrad \cite{yu2020gradient}
modifies gradients to reduce interference. Sener and Koltun
\cite{sener2018multi} proposed the Multi-Gradient Descent Algorithm (MGDA),
which seeks a solution where no task can be improved without hurting another.
Surveys such as \cite{chen2024gradient} discuss these approaches and note that,
while they help balance tasks, they do not support the sequential learning
needed for curricula.

\subsection{The Equilibrium-Disequilibrium Conflict}
Curriculum learning requires the model to focus on easier tasks first and
gradually shift to harder ones, rather than balancing all tasks equally.
Standard balancing methods often fail here because they might upweight the hard
task too early if its gradient magnitude is small, or maintain the easy task's
weight indefinitely. \cite{lanzillotta2025multitask} recently challenged the
assumption that standard MTL objectives are optimal for continual learning,
arguing that static multi-task solutions fail to account for distributional
drift and that dynamic adaptation of the loss landscape is required.

\subsection{Pareto Continual Learning}
Bridging this gap, \cite{lai2025pareto} introduced \textbf{Pareto Continual
	Learning}, which reformulates the stability-plasticity trade-off (retaining old
knowledge vs. learning new tasks) as a Multi-Objective Optimization (MOO)
problem. Instead of finding a single fixed weight, they learn a set of
Pareto-optimal solutions conditioned on a preference vector. This allows the
system to dynamically select the optimal trade-off during inference. Similarly,
\cite{mao2022metaweighting} proposed \textbf{MetaWeighting}, identifying a
critical gap between training loss and generalization loss in MTL. By treating
task weights as hyperparameters optimized via a bi-level optimization loop
(meta-learning), MetaWeighting explicitly minimizes generalization loss. This
distinction is vital for this thesis: we do not merely want to balance the
gradients of the smoothed and sharp losses; we want to schedule them to
maximize final validation performance.

\section{Meta-Learning and Continual Adaptation}
\label{sec:meta-learning-continual}

Meta-learning, or ``learning to learn,'' provides the algorithmic engine to
automate the curriculum schedule. While early meta-learning focused on learning
initializations (e.g., MAML \cite{finn2017model}) for few-shot adaptation, the
field has expanded into \textbf{Meta-Continual Learning (MCL)}.

\subsection{Taxonomy of Meta-Continual Frameworks}
A recent survey by \cite{son2024meta} provides a rigorous taxonomy for the
intersection of these fields. They distinguish between \textit{Continual
	Meta-Learning} (learning to learn from a sequence of tasks) and
\textit{Meta-Continual Learning} (using meta-learning to solve the catastrophic
forgetting problem). Our research aligns with their definition of
\textbf{Continual Bi-Level Learning}, where the optimization process itself is
updated online.

\subsection{Meta-Optimization of Learning Dynamics}
In this framework, the learning process is split into two loops. The inner loop
updates model parameters $\theta$ on the current curriculum stage via standard
gradient descent. The outer loop updates meta-parameters $\phi$ (in our case,
the loss weights) to minimize a validation loss. Algorithms like
\textbf{La-MAML} \cite{gupta2020lamaml} employ this structure to learn
per-parameter learning rates that align gradients across sequential tasks.
\cite{wu2024meta} recently improved upon this with \textbf{VR-MCL}, introducing
variance reduction techniques to stabilize the online Hessian approximation
required for these bi-level updates.

Furthermore, \cite{woo2025meta} extended MCL to \textbf{Neural Fields}
(MCL-NF). They demonstrated that meta-learning could efficiently reconstruct
complex 3D scenes and signals by dynamically modulating the network's capacity,
a process analogous to the resolution-based curriculum proposed in this thesis.

\subsection{The Stability-Plasticity Dilemma in Curriculum}
A unique challenge arises when applying meta-learning to curriculum generation:
the stability-plasticity dilemma. In standard continual learning, the goal is
to prevent the forgetting of old tasks (stability) \cite{parisi2019continual}.
In our curriculum context, the dilemma is inverted: the model must be
``plastic'' enough to abandon the smoothed approximation (the easy task) and
``stable'' enough to converge on the hard task.

\cite{banayeeanzade2025theoretical} provides theoretical insights into overparameterized models in such multi-task setups, suggesting that without explicit regularization, the interplay between model capacity and task similarity determines the success of transfer. Pure meta-learning of loss weights can be unstable; without constraints, a meta-learner might exploit the easy smoothed loss to achieve artificially low training error without ever transitioning to the hard target function. This necessitates the monotonic regularization strategies explored in this thesis.

\subsection{Learning to Reweight Examples for Robust Deep Learning}
In \cite{renLearningReweightExamples2018a} authors showcase the denoising
capability of reweighting training examples in the deep learning. Their method
assumes two datasets, one that is high in volume and noisy, and another one
that is assumed to be unbiased and clean. Their method doesn't require any
additional hyperparameters. The method inspects the descent direction of some
training examples locally on the training loss surface and reweight them
according to their similarity to the descent direction of the validation loss
surface. Their datasets were obtained by perturbin original CIFAR-10 and
CIFAR-100, in one method just by randomly flipping classes to each other, and
other one where labels flip to some specific new "background" class. In the
first setting a lot of noise is added but the distribution of classes stays
largely uniform in the second one we end up with heavily imabalanced dataset
which is more realistic. It turns out that the method they proposed didn't seem
to provide any benefits over different regularization techniques, like adding
random noise to labels, but on the second (imabalanced) setting, using the
clean and balanced validation set and their novel method, resulted in
significantly better results.

\subsection{Coarse-to-Fine Curriculum Learning}
Authors of the paper \cite{stretcuCoarsetoFineCurriculumLearning2021}
distinguish between curriculum learning in input space and in output space and
attempt to explore the latter one. They proposed method of decomposing the
original classification task into a sequence of coarse-to-fine problems. They
did this by clustering the original labels into a hierarchy of similiar
classes, the similiarity was obtained by taking the class embedding, being
parameters of the model trained for couple of epochs in the last layer, just
before each class. They used hierarchical clustering algorithm to create a tree
of similiar labels of arbitrary depth. Their method introduces only one
additional parameter which is number of epochs that the curriculum should take
before the task is trained on the original loss. The time spent on each coarse
task is uniform. Their method consistenly outperformed the baseline in terms of
final validation accuracy. It turned out that there is a sweet spot for when to
stop the curriculum at around 0.9 number of epochs that it would normally take
the network to converge.
\subsection{Sharpness-Aware Minimization for Efficiently Improving Generalization}
The authors of the paper \cite{foretSharpnessAwareMinimizationEfficiently2021} from
google introduced a novel optimization objective allowing to improve generlization 
abilities of deep learning models. They did it by introducing a sharpness metric,
which allows to find model parameters $w$ that not only have low training loss
$L_S(w)$ but also whoose neighborhood have uniformly low training loss value.
They have shown that the generalization ability is bounded in terms of
neighborhood-wise training loss. 

The formula for Sharpness-Aware-Minimization loss is:
$L_S^{\mathrm{SAM}}(w) \triangleq \max_{\|\epsilon\|_p \leq \rho} L_S(w+\epsilon)$
Which basically means, finding such perturbation $\eps$ of weight $w$, which
length is smaller than $\rho$ (the hyperparameter) that yields in the highest
increase of loss function. Flat regions don't have perturbations that would
change the loss greatly while sharp regions do have them. Unfortunately it is
highly inefficient to find the exact value for every training step, so we need
to approximate it. We do it by ascending in the direction of the gradient.

Optimizing this sharpness metric has shown to empirically improve generalization
abilities of deep learning models on vision tasks. In our CL research, we want to
log sharpness metric to see whether utilizing curriculum results in simliar effects
as SAM optimization, i.e reaching flatter local optima.

\section{Synthesis}

The literature reveals a convergence of these three ideas. Continuation methods
(Gaussian smoothing) provide the theoretical justification for creating a
hierarchy of smoothed loss functions to solve non-convex problems
\cite{ilersich2025deep}. Multi-Objective Optimization provides the
architectural framework to compute these losses simultaneously
\cite{chen2024gradient}. Meta-Learning provides the bi-level optimization
machinery to automate the weighting \cite{son2024meta}.

Existing methods fail to fully integrate these components. Heuristic CL lacks
adaptability. Standard MTL (like GradNorm) seeks equilibrium rather than the
sequential directionality required for curricula. Pure meta-learning lacks the
constraints to enforce a monotonic increase in difficulty. This thesis fills
this gap by using regularized meta-learning to dynamically discover the optimal
curriculum trajectory. By enforcing monotonic constraints on the
meta-gradients, the method ensures the stability of the continuation process
while leveraging the adaptability of meta-learning.

\section{Main experimental milestones}

\subsection{Baseline hyperparameter search}

The first major experiment was the sweep pack archived as
\texttt{2026-03-14\_sweep-pack-v1}. It used the configuration files under
\texttt{configs/sweeps/} and covered eight benchmark functions:
\texttt{ackley}, \texttt{levy}, \texttt{bukin}, \texttt{eggholder},
\texttt{franke}, \texttt{peaks}, \texttt{friedman1\_2d}, and
\texttt{friedman2\_2d}. The pack executed 630 Optuna trials, of which 336
finished and 294 were pruned or killed.

The main purpose was to avoid comparing curriculum training against a weak
manual baseline. The sweep showed that MLP architectures were best for most
functions, while \texttt{peaks} preferred SIREN and \texttt{eggholder} preferred
Fourier features. Eggholder remained the hardest benchmark by a large margin.
This result established a stronger baseline for later curriculum comparisons.

\subsection{Curriculum versus single-target training}

The benchmark \texttt{curr\_vs\_single\_20260315\_175826} compared
\texttt{single} and \texttt{curriculum} training using the best hyperparameters
from the sweep pack. It covered eight functions, three seeds and two fairness
regimes: equal epoch budget and equal wall-clock-time budget.

The benchmark completed successfully: 96 out of 96 planned runner jobs finished,
and all 96 corresponding MLflow runs were marked finished. The result was
balanced on final quality but one-sided on runtime:

\begin{itemize}
    \item Under equal epochs, curriculum won 5 function-level quality comparisons
          and single-target training won 3.
    \item Under equal time, curriculum won 3 quality comparisons and
          single-target training won 5.
    \item Across all function/regime cells, curriculum and single training each
          won 8 quality comparisons.
    \item Single-target training won all 16 runtime comparisons.
\end{itemize}

The strongest interpretation is therefore not that curriculum learning is always
better. The safer conclusion is that Gaussian-continuation curriculum is useful
for selected functions, especially \texttt{bukin}, \texttt{franke} and
\texttt{levy}, but it is not a global default. It clearly adds runtime overhead.

\subsection{Ratio-controlled sweeps and training diagnostics}

After the first benchmark, additional v2 and v3 ratio-controlled sweeps were
introduced. Their goal was to control model capacity more carefully through the
train-samples-per-parameter ratio and to log additional optimization diagnostics,
such as gradient statistics, Hessian-trace estimates and sharpness proxies.

The v3 ratio experiments produced many MLflow runs, for example 553 recorded
runs for \texttt{sweep-ackley-v3-ratio} and 270 for
\texttt{sweep-levy-v3-ratio}. These experiments improved the infrastructure and
provided useful diagnostics, but they are less clean as final headline evidence
than the archived sweep-pack v1 and curriculum-vs-single benchmark. They should
be used mainly for methodological discussion and internal model-selection notes.

\subsection{Meta-weighting experiments}

The next stage implemented validation-guided adaptive weighting over multiple
smoothed losses. The code is in \texttt{ma\_thesis/meta\_train.py}. The method
uses softmax-normalized loss weights and updates them using the hard-target
validation loss after a short differentiable lookahead. This is an operational
meta-weighting approach, but it should be described carefully: it is not yet the
full monotonic-gradient-regularized algorithm proposed in the early design notes.

Several meta-weighting campaigns were run in April and May 2026. The clearest
Eggholder analysis is the unroll-1 campaign
\texttt{meta\_weighting\_eggholder\_u1\_20260504\_221357}, which analyzed 288
finished runs. The aggregate result was mixed:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \texttt{num\_losses=1} had the best average final validation loss.
    \item Among true multi-loss variants, \texttt{num\_losses=3} was the most
          promising aggregate setting.
    \item Multi-loss variants had win rates below 50\% against matched
          single-loss baselines in the paired comparison.
    \item The most promising region was around \(\eta_{\mathrm{model}}=0.03\),
          \(\eta_{\mathrm{meta}}=0.003\), and one-step meta lookahead.
\end{itemize}

This is an important negative or mixed result. It shows that adaptive
meta-weighting is feasible and produces meaningful weight trajectories, but it
also shows that the method is sensitive and cannot yet be claimed as a robust
improvement over hard-target training.

\subsection{Policy-based and LLM-generated schedules}

Because direct meta-weighting was unstable, the project added a more modular
policy-based schedule interface. Schedule policies receive a compact training
state and output normalized loss weights. This enabled both handcrafted schedules
and LLM-generated schedules to be evaluated with the same benchmark scripts.

The strongest policy-search result so far is the canonical Eggholder LLM
schedule study compared with the corresponding Eggholder Optuna schedule study.
The best completed LLM candidate had mean best hard validation loss 42680.20,
while the best Optuna trial had 43669.97.
The absolute gap was 989.77 in favor of the LLM schedule. This is promising, but
it should be reported with caveats: only 11 of 20 LLM candidates completed, and
the result should be confirmed before being treated as definitive.

The substantive observation is that the LLM-generated schedules often converged
to interpretable easy-first-to-hard trajectories, whereas Optuna explored a wider
and less stable family of schedules. This gives a useful thesis discussion point:
schedule search can matter more than simply adding more smoothed losses.

\subsection{CIFAR-100 easy-vs-hard weighting}

A second adaptive-schedule track was added for CIFAR-100. The easy task is
coarse superclass prediction, while the hard task is fine-label prediction. This
track is implemented with PyTorch Lightning in
\texttt{ma\_thesis/cifar100\_lightning.py}.

The single-seed comparison \texttt{cifar100\_v1\_llm} versus
\texttt{cifar100\_v1\_optuna} found that the best LLM-generated schedule reached
best hard validation loss 1.3115, while the best Optuna schedule reached 1.3279.
Five of the ten successful LLM candidates beat the best Optuna trial on the
search objective. However, the comparison used only seed 42, and the best Optuna
loss trial had slightly better test accuracy. Therefore this result is promising
but not final.

\subsection{Coarse-to-fine CIFAR-100 reproduction}

In June 2026, a separate PyTorch reproduction of coarse-to-fine curriculum
learning was added under \texttt{coarse-to-fine-curriculum/}. This experiment is
not the original Gaussian-continuation method, but it is useful because it tests
a related curriculum idea in a more standard vision setting.

One completed CIFAR-100 CNN run showed a clear positive result:

\begin{itemize}
    \item Baseline best test accuracy: 0.3625.
    \item Curriculum best test accuracy: 0.4128.
    \item Absolute gain: 0.0503, or 5.03 percentage points.
    \item Relative gain over baseline: 13.88\%.
\end{itemize}

This is currently one of the clearest positive curriculum signals in the
repository. Since it is only one seed and one model, it should be presented as a
promising supporting experiment unless it is repeated across additional seeds.

\section{Current state of the thesis project}

The implementation side of the project is now substantially developed. The
repository contains data generation, training, sweeps, MLflow logging, analysis
scripts, meta-weighting, policy schedules, CIFAR-100 experiments and
coarse-to-fine reproduction tooling. The main scientific picture is also clearer
than at the beginning of the project:

\begin{itemize}
    \item Strong baselines matter. Once hyperparameters are tuned, fixed
          Gaussian-continuation curriculum does not automatically dominate.
    \item Curriculum learning is task-dependent. It helps some benchmark
          functions but hurts or slows others.
    \item Runtime cost is a real limitation. Sequential curriculum was slower in
          every cell of the main curriculum-vs-single benchmark.
    \item Direct meta-weighting is feasible but unstable. The current evidence is
          mixed, especially on Eggholder.
    \item Policy-based schedule search is the most promising adaptive direction,
          especially LLM-generated easy-to-hard schedules, but it still needs
          confirmation.
    \item CIFAR-100 experiments provide useful supporting evidence that
          curriculum can help in output-space or hierarchical settings.
\end{itemize}

The recommended final thesis claim should therefore be cautious: curriculum
learning is not a universal improvement, but schedule design matters, and
adaptive or task-aware schedules can produce gains in selected regimes.

    \item Freeze the final set of experiments and avoid broad new sweeps.
    \item Confirm the most promising CIFAR-100 or policy-search result across
          additional seeds if time permits.
    \item Convert the existing appendices and reports into the main experimental
          chapter.
    \item Clearly separate implemented methods from proposed but not fully
          validated ideas, especially the monotonic meta-regularizer.
    \item Present negative and mixed results explicitly, because they strengthen
          the credibility of the thesis.
\end{enumerate}

\chapter{Systematic Literature Review Protocol}
\chaptermark{SLR Protocol}{}

This chapter presents the protocol for the Systematic Literature Review
(\acronym{SLR}) which serves as the foundation for the research component of
this thesis. The protocol has been developed in accordance with the
\acronym{PRISMA} guidelines and the \acronym{PICOC} framework.

\section{Review Identification}

The objective of this review is to synthesize current methodologies that
utilize \termdef{Meta-Optimization} to dynamically schedule loss functions
representing decomposed sub-tasks of varying difficulty (e.g., via function
approximation, smoothing, or auxiliary objectives) in deep learning scenarios.

\section{The PICOC Framework}

To precisely define the scope of the search, the \acronym{PICOC} framework
(Population, Intervention, Comparison, Outcome, Context) was utilized. Details
are presented in Table~\ref{tab:picoc}.

\section{Research Questions (RQs)}

The following research questions have been defined to guide the data extraction
process:

\begin{description}
	\item[RQ1 (Curriculum)] How do adaptive approaches (e.g., Self-Paced Learning,
	      RL-based teachers) differ from static heuristics in defining training
	      schedules?
	\item[RQ2 (Multi-Task)] What strategies are employed to mitigate negative transfer
	      (e.g., gradient conflict resolution) in hard vs. soft parameter sharing
	      architectures?
	\item[RQ3 (Meta-Optimization)] How are meta-learning algorithms (e.g., MAML,
	      reweighting) applied to optimize loss weights or data selection dynamically?
\end{description}

\section{Search Strategy}

The search strategy relies on the intersection of three conceptual blocks:
\emph{Curriculum Learning}, \emph{Meta-Optimization}, and \emph{Multi-Task
	Learning}. Below are the specific queries designed for scientific databases.

\subsection{Scopus / Web of Science}

This is the primary source due to its broad indexing coverage.

\begin{verbatim}
TITLE-ABS-KEY (
  ("curriculum learning" OR "self-paced learning" OR "loss weighting" 
   OR "adaptive weighting" OR "dynamic weighting") 
  AND ("meta-learning" OR "meta-optimization" OR "learning to weight" 
   OR "gradient-based" OR "differentiable" OR "automated") 
  AND ("multi-task" OR "multi-objective" OR "auxiliary learning" 
   OR "multi-loss" OR "negative transfer")
) AND ( DOCTYPE(ar) OR DOCTYPE(cp) ) AND NOT ( "reinforcement learning" )
\end{verbatim}

\subsection{IEEE Xplore}

A stricter syntax is required here (using the \texttt{"All Metadata"}
operator).

\begin{verbatim}
("All Metadata":"curriculum learning" OR "All Metadata":"self-paced learning" 
 OR "All Metadata":"loss weighting" OR "All Metadata":"adaptive weighting" 
 OR "All Metadata":"dynamic weighting") 
AND ("All Metadata":"meta-learning" OR "All Metadata":"meta-optimization" 
 OR "All Metadata":"learning to weight" OR "All Metadata":"gradient-based" 
 OR "All Metadata":"differentiable" OR "All Metadata":"automated") 
AND ("All Metadata":"multi-task" OR "All Metadata":"multi-objective" 
 OR "All Metadata":"auxiliary learning" OR "All Metadata":"multi-loss")
\end{verbatim}

\subsection{Google Scholar}

Due to limitations in boolean logic handling, the search will be conducted
using shorter, phrases:
\begin{verbatim}
"dynamic weighting" "multi task" "curriculum learning"
\end{verbatim}
due to large number of hits, I chose to only take into consideration papers
released after 2023.

\subsection{Snowballing Strategy}

To complement the database search and ensure comprehensive coverage, a
snowballing procedure will be applied:
\begin{itemize}
	\item \textbf{Forward Snowballing:} Reviewing papers that cite seminal works identified in the supervisor's recommendations (e.g., \emph{GradNorm}, \emph{MAML}, \emph{Self-Paced Learning}) to identify recent advancements.
	\item \textbf{Backward Snowballing:} Examining the reference lists of included primary studies to identify foundational papers or missed relevant studies.
\end{itemize}

\section{Selection Criteria and Data Extraction}

The selection process will be conducted in two stages: title/abstract screening
followed by full-text review.

	\centering
	\resizebox{0.95\textwidth}{!}{
		\input{figures/prisma_flow.tex}
	}
	\fcmfcaption{Schematic of the PRISMA selection process.}\label{rys:prisma}

\paragraph{Inclusion Criteria.} The review will include papers that:
\begin{enumerate}
	\item Propose a method for \emph{automating} the training process (adaptive
	      curriculum or loss weighting).
	\item Address Multi-Task Learning challenges, specifically Negative Transfer or
	      parameter sharing.
	\item Utilize Meta-Learning, Self-Paced Learning, or Reinforcement Learning
	      approaches.
	\item Were published between 2017 and 2025.
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{Data Extraction.} The following information will be extracted from selected papers:
\begin{itemize}
	\item \textbf{Metadata:} Title, year, publication venue.
	\item \textbf{Methodology:} Type of Curriculum (Static/Adaptive/SPL) and Optimization strategy.
	\item \textbf{Architecture:} Hard vs. Soft parameter sharing.
	\item \textbf{Results:} Mitigation of negative transfer and generalization improvements.
\end{itemize}
