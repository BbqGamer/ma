\chapter{Roughness, Dataset Controls, and Adaptive Curriculum Follow-up}
\label{app:roughness-followup-curriculum}

\section{Purpose}

Appendix~\ref{app:cifar100-model-size-curriculum} showed that fixed
coarse-to-fine curriculum learning helped smaller CIFAR-100
\cite{krizhevsky2009learning} models, especially weaker CNNs, but helped less
as model capacity increased. This appendix extends that result in three
directions:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item multi-seed roughness follow-up runs on CIFAR-100,
    \item controls on Fashion-MNIST and CIFAR-10,
    \item an adaptive curriculum policy that advances curriculum stages based on
          validation plateau instead of a hand-picked fixed curriculum length.
\end{enumerate}

The central question is not only whether curriculum helps, but under which
conditions it helps: model capacity, dataset, hierarchy depth, schedule policy,
and optimizer/model choice.

\section{Methods compared}

Two curriculum families are compared.

\paragraph{Fixed curriculum.}
The fixed runs use the best or most relevant curriculum lengths identified in the
previous sweeps, for example \texttt{curr20} for \texttt{cnn\_w0.5},
\texttt{curr10} for \texttt{cnn\_w1}, and \texttt{curr5} for larger CNNs.
These runs answer whether a hand-picked schedule can improve the final task.

\paragraph{Adaptive plateau curriculum.}
The adaptive policy starts at a coarse hierarchy level and monitors fine-label
validation accuracy. It advances to the next finer level when the validation
score plateaus after a minimum number of stage epochs, or when a maximum stage
length is reached. The CIFAR-100 runs used a slower rule: at least 10 epochs per
stage, at most 40, patience 5, and minimum improvement 0.002. The CIFAR-10
\cite{krizhevsky2009learning} and Fashion-MNIST \cite{xiao2017fashion} runs
used a shorter rule: at least 5 epochs per stage, at most 20, patience 3, and
minimum improvement 0.001.

For CIFAR-100, two hierarchy-depth variants were tested:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \texttt{adaptive\_all}: use all non-singleton generated hierarchy levels,
    \item \texttt{adaptive\_max1}: use only the first generated hierarchy level.
\end{itemize}

The adaptive method was designed to reduce manual tuning. A negative result is
therefore still informative: it tells us that simply using validation plateau is
not enough to replace the fixed schedule.

The gains in parentheses are paired against the baseline from the same policy
family and seed. This matters because an adaptive curriculum can have a strong
absolute accuracy but a small gain if its matching adaptive baseline is also
strong.

The main result is clear: fixed hand-picked schedules still outperform the
adaptive plateau policy on CIFAR-100. Adaptive scheduling is not useless: it
still improves \texttt{cnn\_w0.5}, \texttt{cnn\_w1}, and
\texttt{cifar\_resnet8}. However, it fails badly for \texttt{cnn\_w2} and
\texttt{cnn\_w4}. This suggests that the adaptive rule is too conservative or
uses coarse supervision for too long in models that only need a very short
curriculum.

The hierarchy-depth comparison is also important. For the models where both
adaptive variants were run, \texttt{adaptive\_max1} is consistently better than
\texttt{adaptive\_all}. This means that simply using all generated hierarchy
levels is not optimal. The number of hierarchy levels is an experimental design
choice, not a harmless implementation detail.

still much smaller than CIFAR-100. The adaptive policy gives a useful gain for
\texttt{cnn\_w1} and a small gain for \texttt{cnn\_w0.5}; however, trajectory

learning to help. The dataset must also have enough class structure and task
complexity for coarse labels to be useful.

\section{Roughness and sharpness diagnostics}

The roughness results remain mixed. Table~\ref{tab:appg-roughness-cifar100}
shows the CIFAR-100 roughness follow-up. The roughness columns are
\(\log_{10}(\text{curriculum}/\text{baseline})\) ratios, so negative values mean
that the curriculum run had a lower value than the matching baseline, while
positive values mean that it had a higher value.

\begin{table}[h]
\centering
\small
\begin{tabular}{lrrrrr}
\hline
Model & Acc. gain & Final sharp. & Final Hess. & Mean sharp. & Mean Hess. \\
\hline
\texttt{cnn\_w1} & +1.98 & +0.35 & +0.04 & +0.28 & -0.07 \\
\texttt{cnn\_w2} & +1.72 & -0.08 & +0.06 & +0.11 & -0.17 \\
\texttt{cnn\_w4} & +0.34 & +0.15 & +0.23 & +0.20 & -0.04 \\
\texttt{cifar\_resnet8} & +1.33 & -0.16 & -0.30 & -0.04 & -0.71 \\
\hline
\end{tabular}
\caption{CIFAR-100 roughness follow-up for fixed curricula. Accuracy gain is in
percentage points. Roughness and Hessian columns are log-ratios of curriculum to
baseline; negative values indicate lower roughness for the curriculum run.}
\label{tab:appg-roughness-cifar100}
\end{table}

The most favorable case for a flattening interpretation is
\texttt{cifar\_resnet8}: it improves accuracy and lowers several roughness
estimates. The CNNs do not give the same clean story. For example,
\texttt{cnn\_w1} improves peak accuracy but has higher final sharpness, and
\texttt{cnn\_w4} barely improves accuracy while also tending toward higher final
roughness. The adaptive runs do not change this conclusion.

The safest statement is therefore:

\begin{quote}
The experiments confirm a capacity-, dataset-, and schedule-dependent curriculum
effect, but current roughness probes do not prove that the effect is caused by
consistently finding flatter minima.
\end{quote}

\section{Interpretation}

The updated experiments lead to five conclusions.

First, CIFAR-100 remains the strongest positive dataset. The fixed schedules are
still the best-performing method, especially for \texttt{cnn\_w0.5},
\texttt{cnn\_w1}, \texttt{cnn\_w2}, and \texttt{cifar\_resnet8}.

Second, adaptive plateau scheduling is promising as a concept but not yet better
than hand-tuned fixed schedules. It reduces manual curriculum-length selection,
but it can stay in coarse stages too long. This is especially harmful for larger
CNNs, where the best fixed curriculum was very short.

Third, hierarchy depth matters. On CIFAR-100, \texttt{adaptive\_max1} is better
than \texttt{adaptive\_all} for the models where both were tested. This suggests
that future automatic curricula should tune or learn which hierarchy levels to
use, not only when to advance between them.

Fourth, easy 10-class controls do not show the same effect. Fashion-MNIST is
neutral or negative, while CIFAR-10 shows only small CNN gains. This supports the
claim that curriculum is useful only when the dataset has enough class structure
and difficulty.

Fifth, Tiny ImageNet requires a stronger setup. Small models with Adam were
negative, but ResNet-18 with SGD/cosine achieved a modest positive curriculum
gain. This suggests that the failure of the first Tiny ImageNet pilot was not
only a dataset issue; it also depended on model capacity and optimization.

Overall, the thesis-level claim should be narrow and defensible:

\begin{quote}
Coarse-to-fine curriculum learning can improve peak accuracy for lower-capacity
models on structured many-class image classification tasks, but the effect is
not universal. It depends on hierarchy quality, hierarchy depth, model capacity,
dataset difficulty, and schedule design.
\end{quote}
